# Instagram caption — "The robot's cartwheel was a human's first"

This robot just did a cartwheel. A human did it first. 🤸🤖

New research out of Berkeley and Stanford suited people up in motion-capture gear and recorded them walking, running, dancing, doing martial arts and cartwheels — about two and a half hours of human movement in total.

Then a Unitree G1 humanoid had to follow along. It scores points for matching the move, and loses them for jerky motion, bad joint angles, or bumping into itself.

The recorded moves get squeezed into a compact code, and an AI plans brand-new sequences from it: sprints, spin kicks, flips, cartwheels. On the real robot, joystick steering and dodging obstacles worked without retraining.

Does it look human? People compared its walk with Unitree's standard controller — and more than two thirds of the time, they picked the new one as more natural.

Boston Dynamics and the RAI Institute built a similar system, ZEST. It can learn from mocap, ordinary video, or an animator's keyframes — and even a four-legged robot learned a backflip.

The catch, per ZEST's authors: flat, non-slippery floors only, and nothing it hasn't seen.
