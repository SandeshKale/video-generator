## 4. Pick A — "That NASA Diwali photo isn't Diwali"

**Why it can trend:** the image is forwarded every Diwali (fact-checkers have been tracking it since at least 2011–2018), it is *real satellite data with a wrong caption*, and Diwali is 8 days away. People forward it with pride, so a "you've been forwarding a mislabelled photo" reel is send-to-the-uncle material.

### Verified facts

| Fact | Source | Tier |
|---|---|:-:|
| NASA's real Diwali-period image was captured **12 Nov 2012** by **VIIRS (Day-Night Band) on Suomi NPP**, published 15 Nov 2012. Credit: "NASA Earth Observatory image by Jesse Allen and Robert Simmon, using VIIRS Day-Night Band data from the Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership (Suomi NPP)." | NASA Earth Observatory, "South Asian Night Lights" (fetched) | **A** |
| NASA: "any extra light produced during Diwali is so subtle that it is likely imperceptible when observed from space." | same page (fetched) | **A** |
| The viral image is a **composite made in 2003 by NOAA scientist Chris Elvidge** from DMSP satellite data, combining several years, to show population growth. Colour key: white = lights visible before 1992, blue = 1992, green = 1998, red = 2003. It resurfaces every Diwali. | EarthSky 6 Nov 2018 (fetched), Factly, The Quint | B |
| Every November satellites see **smoke from crop-residue burning** over the Indo-Gangetic Plain; the haze is trapped between the Himalayas and the Vindhyas, and temperature inversions act "like a lid". Fireworks are named as *one* contributor. Image: 3 Nov 2024, VIIRS/Suomi NPP. NASA's Hiren Jethva forecast 15,500–18,500 fires for 2024. | NASA Earth Observatory, "Smoky Skies Over the Indo-Gangetic Plain" (fetched) | **A** |
| ISRO/NRSC night-lights atlas: India's night-light radiance rose **43% between 2012 and 2021** (Bihar +474%, Manipur +441%, Ladakh +280%, Kerala +119%); built from NASA/NOAA data. | India Infra Hub summary of the NRSC **NTL Atlas** | B (get the ISRO PDF: `isro.gov.in/media_isro/pdf/Publications/book/NTL_Atlas.pdf`) |
| Diwali 2025: Delhi's post-Diwali fine-particle average was the **worst since 2021** even though stubble burning was sharply down (reports: Punjab+Haryana 175 fires vs 779 over 1–12 Oct 2025; "77.5% drop"). | Climate Trends / CPCB data via Deccan Herald, NE Herald, Down To Earth | B |
| Diwali 2026 = **Sunday 8 Nov** (Dhanteras Fri 6 Nov, Choti Diwali Sat 7 Nov). Lakshmi Puja times differ by city. | 5+ panchang sites agree | B (only the date is used) |
| 2026 cracker rules for Delhi are **not published yet**; the Supreme Court asked CPCB in Aug 2026 to consider relaxing noise limits. | The Wire, Hindutone | C — do not state a rule |

### Do NOT claim (traps)
- That satellites "saw firecracker smoke". NASA's pages credit crop burning; none isolates fireworks. Say "satellites see November smoke; fireworks are one ingredient".
- Any specific Delhi PM2.5/AQI number from this list until it is checked against a CPCB bulletin (the "488 vs 156.6" figure had an unclear unit).
- That LED festival lights would show up: one researcher notes VIIRS's night sensor is poor at blue-white LED light — single source (tier C), so mention as "the sensor sees street lighting best", not as fact.
- "Fake". It is **real data, wrong caption**. Say mislabelled, not hoax.

### Story spine (cause chain, one object the viewer owns: the forwarded photo)
1. **Hook (0–3 s):** the forwarded photo on a phone, pinch-zoom, then the line "यह फ़ोटो दिवाली की नहीं है।"
2. **Plant:** "Main ek tareekh batata hoon — 8 November. Uske baad wahi satellite kya dekhta hai, woh bhi dikhaunga." (setup for Part 2)
3. **Reveal the legend:** the colours are **years** (white pre-1992, blue 1992, green 1998, red 2003) — the "Diwali glow" is cities appearing over 11 years.
4. **What NASA's real Diwali-week image shows:** Suomi NPP, 12 Nov 2012, and NASA's own sentence: any extra Diwali light is imperceptible.
5. **Why:** a satellite pixel is huge; lamps are tiny. (Use the grid-cells part: one light pixel vs one street.)
6. **The real India-at-night story:** +43% radiance 2012→2021 (ISRO atlas).
7. **What satellites *do* see in November:** smoke trapped against the Himalayas.
8. **Payoff line + CTA:** "Forward this to the person who forwarded you the photo." + LIKE · SHARE · SUBSCRIBE.

## 6. Pick B — NavIC is back (India's own GPS)

**Why it can trend:** fresh news (launch **30 Oct 11:51 pm IST**), pride, and a real drama: *the clocks failed*.

| Fact | Source | Tier |
|---|---|:-:|
| **GSLV-F18 lifted off 30 Oct 2026, 11:51 pm IST** from Sriharikota's second launch pad (GSLV's 20th flight), all three stages performed; **NVS-03** (2,232 kg, second-generation) was injected into GTO (planned 170 × 37,023 km) and will reach its slot at **32.5°E**. | The Tribune 31 Oct (fetched), Telangana Today | B (ISRO post-launch release not found yet) |
| NVS-03 carries an **indigenous rubidium atomic clock** and transmits **L1, L5 and S**; NavIC is restored to **four** operational satellites, "the minimum operational configuration". ISRO chief: "one nation, one time"; a **56-day** turnaround between GSLV flights. | Tribune; ISRO mission page (L1 added; coverage India + 1,500 km) | B/A |
| **IRNSS-1F's last atomic clock stopped on 13 Mar 2026** (some outlets say 10 Mar), days after its 10-year design life ended; it had three clocks and two had already failed. | GPS World, ThePrint, ISRO statement as reported | B |
| In **July 2026** the government said NavIC could no longer give navigation data on its own. | The Week 31 Jul 2026, Superkalam | B |
| History: IRNSS-1A's rubidium clocks failed (announced 2017); the 1H replacement failed to reach orbit (31 Aug 2017); early clocks were **imported** (SpectraTime); NVS-01 (May 2023) carried the first indigenous clock; **NVS-02 (Jan 2025) did not reach its intended orbit**. | Wikipedia/ThePrint/GPS World | B |
| "Six of 11 NavIC satellites have failed since 2013"; "a smartphone mandate exists" | single outlets, conflicting | **C — do not use** |

**Physics to show honestly:** a position fix needs **four** satellites (x, y, z and the receiver's clock error), which is *why* "minimum 4" matters and *why* the clock is the whole story. NavIC is geostationary/geosynchronous (≈36,000 km), not low orbit.
**Assets:** `nasa/` satellite models (TDRS / GOES as **labelled stand-ins**, since no NavIC model exists — say "illustrative"), `earth.py` Earth with India centred, HUD `rayCast` (four rays to a phone dot), `barChart`/gauge for "satellites working: 4 → 3 → 4", `smartphone.glb` with a `ui-kit` map, `caption-rig`, `kenney` sounds.
**New technique:** the four-ray solve — rays arrive one at a time, the position error circle collapses with each, and shrinks to a dot only at the fourth.
**Risk:** the clock-failure counts differ by outlet; stick to the tier-B items. The Smartphone support and "mandate" lines stay out.
